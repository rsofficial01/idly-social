## #1723: fact check and draft (for the next run)

idly's two sources are InsideEVs and Motor1, and both were read. They confirm 36,000 first-year (2021 model
year) Mach-Es, an average of about 92% battery health, and that the data is Ford's own. Two problems for the
desk:

- **The headline overstates it.** The 92% is the average across all 36,000 cars, not the figure "after 100,000
  miles". InsideEVs puts the cohort's average at about 55,000 miles over 5.2 years. The 100,000-mile figure
  applies to a subset of more than 2,000 cars, which averaged better than 91%.
- **One line is wrong.** The article says "Ford has not said how many of the 36,000 cars were driven past
  100,000 miles". Both sources say Ford put it at more than 2,000.

The draft card avoided both. The cover headline was "Ford says its first Mach-Es still hold 92% battery
health", which uses only the article's figures and links the 100,000 miles to Ford's "hold up well" claim
from the headline, not to the 92%. The draft caption is below. It isn't scored by a reviewer, and its photo
credit is still a placeholder:

```
Ford says the batteries in its first-year Mustang Mach-E electric SUVs are holding up better than many buyers expect. Across the 36,000 cars it examined, the average battery still had about 92% of its original health, which means it holds roughly 92% of the capacity it had when new.

Ford says the batteries hold up well even after 100,000 miles (about 1.6 lakh km). These are the company's own figures, not an independent test. The 92% is an average, not a guarantee for any single car: how a battery is charged and driven can make it age faster or slower.

Ford hasn't said whether it will publish the full data, or how the results compare with rival electric SUVs.
```
